Scope and method

This chapter is a source-faithful integration of only the four uploaded books. No web source, guideline update, general medical knowledge, or external drug reference has been added. The language is intentionally simple, but the medical content, thresholds, doses, mechanisms, exceptions, controversies, and source disagreements are preserved. A statement is merged only when the source material supports the same clinical meaning. If the books differ, the difference is shown instead of silently choosing one version.

  2. Textbook of Critical Care, 7th edition, 2017.
  3. The Washington Manual of Critical Care, 3rd edition, 2018.

All compact references use [book number.PDF page]. Example: [1.1233] means reference 1, PDF page 1233. A range such as [1.1234-1236] means pages 1234 through 1236.

For completeness, the core sepsis chapters were merged with sepsis-specific material from the books’ chapters on fluid therapy, antimicrobial stewardship, acute kidney injury, adrenal insufficiency, coagulation, and extracorporeal blood purification. Pure index mentions or places where the word “sepsis” is only an incidental example without adding a distinct sepsis-specific fact are not duplicated. Source-specific infection chapters such as pneumonia, meningitis, urosepsis, or abdominal infection remain separate disease topics; their full disease-specific antimicrobial regimens should be integrated when those diseases are built as their own chapters. The general sepsis antibiotic algorithm is included here because it is presented specifically as “Antibiotic Management of Sepsis and Septic Shock.” [3.28]

Clinical-use note: This document reproduces and integrates what the four books state. It is not an independent current guideline. Older algorithms and edition-specific recommendations are clearly labeled so that historical recommendations are not mistaken for a single unified current standard.

Definitions and terminology

Sepsis

Sepsis is defined as life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection. Organ dysfunction is identified as an increase in the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score of at least 2 points.[3.26]

The definition is important because infection alone is not sufficient; the clinical problem is infection accompanied by clinically important organ dysfunction caused by an abnormal host response.[3.26]

Septic shock

Septic shock is defined as sepsis requiring vasopressors to maintain a mean arterial pressure (MAP) of at least 65 mmHg together with lactate above 2 mmol/L in the absence of hypovolemia.[3.26]

Septic shock can be described clinically as persistent severe arterial hypotension that requires vasopressor support despite adequate fluid resuscitation, together with perfusion abnormalities such as oliguria, reduced peripheral perfusion, or altered mental status. Hyperlactatemia above approximately 2 mEq/L is typical.[1.1233]

A closely aligned clinical description is: persistent hypotension requiring vasopressors despite adequate fluid resuscitation plus impaired perfusion, with lactate commonly above 2 mEq/L.[2.1051-1052]

Bacteremia is not the same as sepsis

Bacteremia is defined as positive blood cultures, which may be transient. The older sepsis framework required clinical evidence of infection plus a systemic response. It also explicitly states that positive blood cultures are not required for the sepsis syndrome.[4.200]

Older terminology

An older framework should be preserved as historical source content rather than merged into the later Sepsis-3 framework. It describes:

  • Sepsis: suspected infection plus tachypnea, tachycardia, and abnormal temperature.
  • Sepsis syndrome: sepsis plus evidence of altered organ perfusion, including PaO2/FiO2 at or below 280 in the absence of another major cardiopulmonary cause, lactate above normal, oliguria below 0.5 mL/kg/h for at least 1 hour, or acute mental-status change.
  • Early septic shock: the sepsis syndrome plus hypotension, defined as systolic pressure below 90 mmHg or a fall of 40 mmHg from baseline, lasting less than 1 hour and responding to conventional treatment.
  • Refractory septic shock: the same hypotension persisting for more than 1 hour despite adequate volume resuscitation and requiring vasopressors.[4.200]
Epidemiology and outcome burden

Approximately 15% of ICU patients develop septic shock at some point, and mortality is about 40%-50%. Mortality in therapeutic trials may appear lower than in routine practice because high-risk groups such as patients with cirrhosis, immunosuppression, or do-not-resuscitate orders are often excluded.[1.1232]

Similarly, septic shock is reported in about 15% of ICU patients, with mortality close to 50%.[2.1050]

Approximately 750,000 cases of sepsis per year in the United States are reported, with a mortality range of 30%-50% that increases with age.[3.26]

Using older data and definitions, more than 750,000 annual cases are reported, with mortality of 20%-60%.[4.200]

Etiology and source of infection

Septic shock is most often bacterial, but fungal, parasitic, and viral infections can also cause it. In about one-third of patients no responsible infectious agent is identified, and about half of the infections described are nosocomial.[1.1232]

The common infection sources listed are:

Source Approximate proportion
Lung 40%
Abdomen 20%
Indwelling venous/arterial catheters and primary bacteremia 15%
Urinary tract 10%

Infection can originate from any site.[1.1232]

Pathophysiology

Recognition of pathogens and danger signals

The sepsis response begins when the host recognizes an invading organism or microbial products. Important microbial factors include gram-negative bacterial lipopolysaccharide (LPS), lipoteichoic acid, and peptidoglycan. Some Staphylococcus aureus and group A beta-hemolytic streptococcal infections may produce superantigens and toxic shock syndrome.[1.1232]

A more detailed blood-purification section describes the immune trigger using pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs). PAMPs are pathogen-derived molecular signatures. DAMPs are host molecules released by tissue injury. Both can activate pattern-recognition receptors (PRRs) on innate immune cells and drive cytokine production.[1.1071-1072]

Cellular and cytokine response

The early humoral response involves the complement system and the contact/kinin-kallikrein system. Monocytes, macrophages, and polymorphonuclear neutrophils recognize pathogens and release mediators that activate other cells. Toll-like receptors are among the receptors involved. Intracellular signaling activates transcription factors including NF-kappaB, leading to proinflammatory cytokine production.[1.1232]

Important cytokines specifically identified include TNF-alpha and IL-1. Secondary mediators include lipid mediators, oxygen free radicals, proteases, and arachidonic-acid metabolites.[1.1232]

IL-6 and IL-17 are also described among the major cytokines involved in dysregulated septic inflammation.[1.1071]

Nitric oxide and vasodilation

Endothelial cells release vasodilators including nitric oxide (NO) and prostaglandins. NO is a powerful vascular smooth-muscle vasodilator and contributes to the early fall in vascular tone. The inflammatory response also releases vasoconstrictor mediators including thromboxane and endothelins, so the circulation contains competing vasoactive signals.[1.1232]

An additional cellular detail is that excessive NO can inhibit mitochondrial respiration directly and can react with superoxide to form peroxynitrite, which interferes with mitochondrial respiratory pathways and may deplete cellular ATP.[2.1050]

Endothelial injury, capillary leak, and edema

Activated leukocytes adhere to vascular endothelium and migrate into subendothelial tissues. Abnormal endothelial junctions increase capillary permeability, causing fluid to leave the vascular space and producing generalized edema.[1.1232]

This capillary leak contributes to the relative and absolute hypovolemia of septic shock and later complicates fluid therapy because additional fluid can worsen tissue edema.[1.1234-1236]

Coagulation, anticoagulant pathways, and fibrinolysis

Proinflammatory mediators create a procoagulant state. Sepsis reduces natural anticoagulants including protein C, protein S, and antithrombin through reduced synthesis and increased consumption/clearance. It also increases plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 (PAI-1), suppressing fibrinolysis. The net effect favors microvascular thrombosis and can progress to disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), contributing to microcirculatory failure and multiple-organ failure.[1.1232]

The coagulation material expands this mechanism. Cytokines increase tissue-factor expression on macrophages and endothelial cells. TNF-alpha, IL-6, and IL-1 are specifically linked to this response. Endothelial injury simultaneously reduces protein C, protein S, antithrombin, and tissue factor pathway inhibitor. Early fibrinolytic activation may be followed quickly by fibrinolytic shutdown as PAI-1 rises.[1.178]

Immunosuppression and immunoparalysis

Sepsis is not only excessive inflammation. Anti-inflammatory mediators including IL-4 and IL-10 are released and may produce a state of relative immunosuppression called immunoparalysis. Some patients are already immunosuppressed at the time sepsis is diagnosed.[1.1232]

A two-phase model is described in the blood-purification material: an early dysregulated proinflammatory phase associated with tissue injury, organ failure, and early mortality, followed or accompanied by an anti-inflammatory phase associated with immunoparalysis, nosocomial infections, viral reactivation, and late mortality. It also describes reduced antigen-presenting-cell function, reduced monocyte HLA-DR expression, lymphocyte apoptosis, lymphopenia, and IL-10 production as features of septic immunosuppression.[1.1071]

PIRO framework

The PIRO framework can be used to characterize septic shock.[1.1232-1233]

P - Predisposition: patient characteristics such as previous health, long-term immunosuppressive therapy, alcoholism, age, sex, and genetic factors can influence susceptibility, disease course, and response to treatment.[1.1232-1233]

I - Infectious insult: the organism, gram-positive versus gram-negative status, bacterial versus viral or fungal etiology, infection source, and the anatomical extent of infection can affect severity and treatment response.[1.1233]

R - Host response: the inflammatory response varies between patients and over time. White blood cell count, C-reactive protein (CRP), and procalcitonin are listed as examples of measurements that may reflect this response.[1.1233]

O - Organ dysfunction: organ dysfunction can be quantified with SOFA, allowing individual organs and the total score to be followed during the illness.[1.1233]

Clinical presentation

Sepsis does not always move through a predictable sequence. Septic shock may appear abruptly without clear preceding hours of obvious sepsis.[1.1233; 2.1051]

Temperature

Fever can be a clue but is not specific. Importantly, fever may be absent. hypothermia occurs in about 10%-15% of septic-shock cases and is associated with higher mortality.[1.1233]

Heart rate, respiratory rate, and white blood cells

Tachycardia is nonspecific because it occurs in many forms of shock. Leukocytosis is also nonspecific. Acute leukopenia may occur because activated leukocytes become trapped in the peripheral circulation and is associated with a worse prognosis. Lactic acidosis may drive compensatory hyperventilation, so tachypnea is also not specific for septic shock.[1.1233]

Perfusion abnormalities

Important perfusion abnormalities described across the sepsis chapters include hypotension, oliguria, poor peripheral perfusion, altered mental status, and hyperlactatemia.[1.1233; 4.200]

Hyperdynamic pattern

A common hemodynamic pattern after intravascular filling is low systemic vascular resistance with relatively high cardiac output. This pattern is not unique to septic shock, but It should increase suspicion when the clinical context fits.[1.1233-1234]

An increase in SOFA of at least 2 points is used as the organ-dysfunction criterion for sepsis.[3.26]

  • altered mentation,
  • systolic blood pressure <=100 mmHg.

The presence of at least two is presented as identifying patients with a higher risk of poor outcome.[3.26]

Hemodynamic changes

The fall in blood pressure in septic shock is multifactorial and includes intense inflammatory vasodilation, increased vascular capacity, external fluid losses such as vomiting, diarrhea, or sweating, increased capillary permeability, and reduced myocardial contractility.[1.1234]

Sepsis-related myocardial depression involves endothelial dysfunction, beta-adrenergic receptor changes, myocardial calcium-handling changes, and negative inotropic mediators including TNF-alpha, IL-1, oxygen free radicals, platelet-activating factor, and NO. The myocardial depression can be reversible when septic shock resolves.[1.1234]

After volume filling, vascular tone remains low and systemic vascular resistance may be reduced while cardiac output is raised. Ejection fraction can fall because contractility is reduced, while stroke volume and cardiac output can be maintained by larger diastolic volumes. Thus myocardial dysfunction may be present even without low cardiac output.[1.1234]

Monitoring

Arterial pressure

A patient with septic shock requires an arterial catheter for reliable continuous blood-pressure assessment and easier repeated blood sampling, including arterial blood gases.[1.1234]

Changes in systolic and pulse pressure during the ventilator cycle may predict response to a fluid challenge in selected mechanically ventilated patients, but the sign is unreliable when the patient triggers the ventilator.[1.1234]

Echocardiography and cardiac-output monitoring

There is a major shift away from routine pulmonary-artery catheter use toward less-invasive monitoring, especially echocardiography. Echocardiography helps visualize ventricular filling and ejection, but it gives intermittent information and cannot by itself determine whether the measured cardiac output is adequate for the patient’s metabolic needs.[1.1234]

Other named techniques include PiCCO, LiDCO, transesophageal Doppler, bioimpedance, and bioreactance. A cardiac-output number in isolation is not sufficient for most critically ill patients.[1.1234]

A somewhat more permissive role is given to the pulmonary-artery catheter in complex cases, with potential use for pulmonary artery occlusion pressure, cardiac output, and mixed venous oxygen saturation (SvO2), although it also states that outcome benefit has not been conclusively demonstrated.[2.1052]

Lactate and tissue perfusion

A normal blood lactate concentration is approximately 1 mEq/L and considers values above 2 mEq/L clearly abnormal in this context.[1.1234]

In septic shock, elevated lactate is not explained only by cellular hypoxia. Additional mechanisms include increased cellular metabolism, inhibition of pyruvate dehydrogenase, and reduced lactate clearance as additional mechanisms.[1.1234]

Repeated lactate measurements can help assess treatment response and have prognostic value. However, because lactate changes relatively slowly, lactate alone should not be used as a moment-to-moment guide to resuscitation.[1.1234]

Regional and microcirculatory monitoring is also discussed. Gastric mucosal pH/PCO2 techniques reflect splanchnic perfusion but are affected by technical factors and are not used clinically in this context. Sublingual microvascular imaging, near-infrared spectroscopy, tissue oxygen saturation, and cutaneous Doppler can demonstrate abnormal microcirculation, and these abnormalities are associated with prognosis, but their routine role in directing early resuscitation remains uncertain.[1.1234-1235]

Management: overall structure

Management is divided into three inseparable components:

  1. effective treatment of the infection,
  2. cardiovascular resuscitation,
  3. modulation/support of the host response.

These are handled in parallel rather than as isolated sequential tasks.[1.1235]

Similar priorities are listed in older terminology: early recognition, cardiopulmonary support, fluid resuscitation, pressor agents, empiric antibiotics, investigation of immunotherapies, steroid replacement when true adrenal insufficiency is present, and drainage of infection foci.[4.201]

Infection treatment and source control

Cultures and microbiologic sampling

Blood and other appropriate material should be sampled for culture. In severe cases, including septic shock, antibiotic therapy is urgent; in less severe or diagnostically complex cases, limited additional evaluation and infectious-disease consultation may help select the optimal agent.[1.1235]

Empiric broad-spectrum therapy

When the organism is not yet known, empiric broad-spectrum antimicrobial therapy is recommended with sufficient breadth to cover likely pathogens. Choice should reflect local flora and resistance patterns. Empiric therapy should then be modified when culture and susceptibility results become available.[1.1235]

Appropriate initial antimicrobial therapy is defined as a regimen with in-vitro activity against the pathogen causing the infection. Inappropriate initial therapy lacks such activity and is associated with treatment failure and worse outcomes. In the severe-sepsis/septic-shock cohort described, the number needed to treat with appropriate antimicrobial therapy to prevent one death was reported as 4.0.[1.1132]

An initial empiric regimen should cover all likely bacterial, fungal, or viral pathogens when relevant and should achieve adequate concentrations in the tissues believed to be the infection source. Selection should consider drug intolerance, recent antibiotics, underlying disease, the clinical syndrome, community and hospital susceptibility patterns, and pathogens previously known to colonize or infect the patient.[1.1132]

Timing of antibiotics

Delays in effective antimicrobial therapy are associated with worse outcomes in serious infections including septic shock. However, automatic broad-spectrum antibiotic treatment for every patient with only possible sepsis creates risks when infection is absent or when the selected drug is not appropriate. This preserves the tension between rapid treatment of true severe infection and avoiding unnecessary antibiotic exposure.[1.1132-1133]

The sepsis-bundle material describes empiric broad-spectrum antibiotics within 1 hour based on the presumed infection site, together with early fluid resuscitation and source control.[1.178]

General antibiotic-selection algorithm

A general algorithm titled “Antibiotic Management of Sepsis and Septic Shock” is used as a decision framework rather than as one universal fixed regimen.[3.28]

Step 1: Is the patient immunocompromised?

Examples of immunocompromise include:

  • HIV positive,
  • neutropenia,
  • chronic corticosteroid therapy,
  • malnutrition,
  • receiving chemotherapy.

If yes, the algorithm advises infectious-disease consultation and notes that treatment may need coverage directed at opportunistic pathogens in addition to bacterial pathogens.[3.28]

Step 2: If not immunocompromised, assess for healthcare-associated risk

Risk factors listed include:

  • recent hospitalization,
  • residence in a nursing home or rehabilitation facility,
  • regular visits to a hospital clinic or dialysis unit,
  • home infusion therapy or wound therapy.[3.28]

If these factors are present, the algorithm asks the clinician to consider potentially resistant nosocomial pathogens including MRSA, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Acinetobacter species, ESBL-positive Klebsiella pneumoniae, and ESBL-positive Escherichia coli.[3.28]

The broad-coverage options are:

  • a broad-spectrum cephalosporin: cefepime, ceftazidime, ceftolozane-tazobactam, or ceftazidime-avibactam; or
  • a carbapenem: imipenem, meropenem, or doripenem; or
  • a beta-lactam/beta-lactamase inhibitor: piperacillin-tazobactam;

plus one of:

  • a fluoroquinolone: ciprofloxacin or levofloxacin; or
  • an aminoglycoside: gentamicin, tobramycin, or amikacin;

plus an MRSA-directed agent:

  • vancomycin, linezolid, tigecycline, or daptomycin, with the algorithm explicitly marking daptomycin as nonpneumonia use.[3.28]

Step 3: If healthcare-associated risk factors are absent

Community pathogens listed include Streptococcus pneumoniae, Escherichia coli, Legionella pneumophila, methicillin-susceptible Staphylococcus aureus, Haemophilus influenzae, and Klebsiella pneumoniae.[3.28]

The algorithm then lists single-agent options:

  • ceftriaxone,
  • a fluoroquinolone: levofloxacin, moxifloxacin, or ciprofloxacin,
  • ampicillin-sulbactam,
  • ertapenem,
  • or a macrolide: azithromycin, clarithromycin, or telithromycin.[3.28]

After microbiologic identification and susceptibility testing, the algorithm instructs clinicians to modify and/or narrow the regimen.[3.28]

Important limitation: The general sepsis algorithm supplies drug names/classes but does not provide a complete universal antibiotic-dose table on these pages. The four books place most antibiotic doses inside infection-specific chapters. Those dose tables should remain in the pneumonia, abdominal sepsis, urinary infection, meningitis, catheter infection, and other source-specific chapters rather than being converted into a false “one-dose-fits-all sepsis regimen.”

Antibiotic pharmacokinetics and dosing in critical illness

Correct drug selection is not enough; adequate concentration at the infection site is required. Beta-lactams and carbapenems are time-dependent agents, so activity is related to the time that free drug concentration remains above the organism’s minimum inhibitory concentration (TFREE/MIC).[1.1133]

Important ICU factors that can change antimicrobial pharmacokinetics include hypoalbuminemia, large-volume crystalloid administration, large pleural effusions, ascites, catecholamines, augmented renal clearance, and renal-replacement therapy.[1.1133]

Prolonged beta-lactam/carbapenem infusions may improve drug exposure, and several meta-analyses suggested better outcomes, although the largest multicenter trial cited did not show a mortality benefit. Prolonged infusion is therefore not presented as universally superior.[1.1133]

Augmented renal clearance (ARC) is defined as an 8-hour creatinine clearance of at least 130 mL/min/1.73 m2. ARC is associated with subtherapeutic beta-lactam and glycopeptide concentrations and may require dosing adjustments or extended infusion. Renal-replacement therapy can also cause underexposure and requires careful adjustment.[1.1134]

Therapeutic drug monitoring can be used for selected antimicrobials. Routine TDM is established more clearly for drugs such as vancomycin, aminoglycosides, and voriconazole than for most beta-lactams, although beta-lactam monitoring is an area of active practice and research.[1.1134]

Duration, reassessment, and de-escalation

For many critically ill patients an empiric course of approximately 7-8 days is sufficient, but longer courses may be required for specific infections such as bacteremia, fungemia, endocarditis, osteomyelitis, or meningitis. Difficult nonfermenting gram-negative organisms, especially Pseudomonas aeruginosa in VAP, may also require longer treatment depending on infection site, host factors, and drug exposure.[1.1134]

Procalcitonin can sometimes reduce antibiotic exposure, but mixed trial results are reported; some studies shortened therapy, whereas other large trials did not reduce total antibiotic days. Daily critical review of the need, spectrum, concentration, and duration of every antimicrobial is recommended.[1.1134]

The de-escalation approach begins with adequate empiric coverage in a high-risk patient and narrows treatment once microbiology and clinical response are known. Initial combinations may need coverage for nonfermenting gram-negative bacilli and MRSA; selected presentations may also require consideration of Candida or Clostridioides difficile. The regimen should then be narrowed to susceptibilities and the actual clinical syndrome.[1.1134-1135]

The ICU antimicrobial algorithm instructs clinicians to obtain microbiologic samples, special stains when needed, biomarkers such as procalcitonin, and rapid microbiologic tests; start empiric therapy based on likely multidrug-resistant organisms, local epidemiology, and patient risk; and monitor temperature, WBC count, hemodynamics, organ function, and biomarkers. When microbiology returns, therapy is reassessed. If only viruses are identified, the algorithm says to consider stopping antibiotics. If the patient improves, it advises de-escalation and a duration guided by site and pathogen; if the patient fails to improve, it advises reviewing dose adequacy, reculturing for superinfection, considering noninfectious causes, and looking for an abscess or uncontrolled source.[1.1137]

Source control

An infected focus must be removed or drained without delay. If the source is not obvious, a systematic search of the “big five”: lungs, abdomen, urine, wounds, and catheters is recommended.[1.1235]

Drainage of any infection focus is also a treatment priority.[4.201]

Cardiovascular resuscitation: the VIP framework

Resuscitation is organized using VIP: Ventilation, Infusion, Pump.[1.1235]

V - Ventilation and oxygenation

All patients with septic shock should have hypoxemia corrected. Severe cases may require endotracheal intubation and invasive mechanical ventilation. Noninvasive ventilation is not recommended for a severely hemodynamically unstable septic-shock patient.[1.1235]

Mechanical ventilation can improve gas exchange and reduce oxygen consumption by the respiratory muscles. Once the circulation becomes more stable, Hyperoxemia should be avoided; an approximate target of a practical SpO2 target of approximately 94%-97%.[1.1235]

I - Infusion: why the patient is hypovolemic

Both absolute and relative hypovolemia are described. Mechanisms include external losses such as vomiting, diarrhea, and sweating; internal fluid loss from increased capillary permeability with edema and pleural/peritoneal effusions; and an expanded vascular capacity caused by arterial and venous dilatation.[1.1235]

Hypovolemia should be corrected because it compromises both cardiac output and peripheral perfusion.[1.1235]

Fluid resuscitation in detail

Clinical endpoints of adequate filling

Assessment is mainly clinical. Useful signs are restoration of arterial pressure, better skin perfusion, improved urine output, and improved mental state. Central venous pressure can be used as one guide, but no single CVP target can be predetermined for every patient.[1.1235]

Cardiac filling pressures should mainly act as a safety limit against excessive fluid. When cardiac output is monitored, a bolus should produce an increase; fluid should stop when additional boluses no longer increase output.[1.1235]

Passive leg raising

Passive leg raising is described as a reversible “mini-fluid challenge.” The relevant response is a transient change in stroke volume rather than blood pressure alone. The maneuver can be misleading if it triggers a stress response that independently raises heart rate, blood pressure, and output.[1.1235]

Fluid-challenge example

The the later septic-shock chapter provides an example TROL fluid-challenge table:

  • Type: Ringer’s lactate.
  • Rate: 200 mL over 10 minutes.
  • Objective: MAP above 75 mmHg.
  • Limit: CVP 16 mmHg.

This is an example fluid-challenge framework, not a universal fixed prescription for every patient.[1.1235]

Older protocolized fluid algorithm

An older protocolized resuscitation algorithm is presented. The entry criteria shown are a clinical picture of sepsis plus one or more of the following: systolic BP below 90 mmHg, MAP below 60 mmHg, lactate at least 2 mmol/L, or ScvO2 below 70%.[3.27]

The algorithm then gives an initial fluid bolus of at least 20 mL/kg using 0.9% NaCl, lactated Ringer’s solution, or 5% albumin.[3.27]

Its stated resuscitation goals are:

  • MAP >=60 mmHg,
  • systolic BP >=90 mmHg,
  • CVP >=8 mmHg,
  • ScvO2 >=70%.[3.27]

If CVP remains below 8 mmHg, the algorithm repeats fluid boluses of at least 20 mL/kg using the same options. If MAP remains below 60 mmHg after the filling target is reached, norepinephrine is given. If ScvO2 remains below 70%, the algorithm instructs clinicians to transfuse if hemoglobin is below 8 g/dL and to use dobutamine if cardiac index is below 3.5 L/min/m2. If all goals are achieved but vasopressors are still needed, adjunctive therapies are considered.[3.27]

This algorithm reflects the protocolized goal-directed approach of its edition. Later discussion of ProMISe, ARISE, and ProCESS acknowledges that these trials did not show improved outcome from a fixed EGDT protocol compared with contemporary standard care.[3.28]

30 mL/kg statement from AKI material

High-quality sepsis resuscitation includes an initial 30 mL/kg bolus within the first 3 hours, followed by frequent assessment with dynamic measures of fluid responsiveness to decide whether additional fluid is needed.[1.1092]

The coagulation material also reproduces the sepsis-bundle concept of 30 mL/kg crystalloid in shock or high lactate, completed within 3 hours, followed by vasopressors if pressure does not respond to initial crystalloid therapy and source control as soon as feasible.[1.178]

Main classes of IV volume therapy

1 divides volume therapy into three broad categories:

  1. Crystalloids - normal saline (NS), lactated Ringer’s (LR), Hartmann’s solution, and balanced salt solutions such as Plasma-Lyte.
  2. Colloids - albumin, hydroxyethyl starch (HES), dextran, and gelatin.
  3. Blood products - including packed red blood cells when volume repletion is needed in the setting of hypoperfusion and inadequate circulating volume.[1.1080-1081]

Crystalloids versus colloids

Fluid quantity may be more important than fluid type, and crystalloids often require greater volume than colloids because they leak more readily into the extravascular space. Colloids are more expensive and carry their own risks.[1.1235-1236]

There is no robust general signal favoring albumin or another colloid over crystalloid as first-line treatment for most hypovolemia or septic shock; crystalloids remain the treatment of choice in most settings.[1.1082-1083]

Similarly, IV crystalloids should generally be used before colloids or blood products in sepsis unless there is active bleeding or symptomatic anemia.[2.951]

Normal saline versus balanced crystalloids

0.9% saline is hyperchloremic relative to plasma. Experimental and human physiologic data link high chloride exposure to renal vasoconstriction, reduced renal cortical perfusion, reduced plasma renin activity, and hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis.[1.1083]

In the SMART trial sepsis subgroup, balanced crystalloid use was associated with favorable outcomes compared with saline, including improved in-hospital 30-day mortality and favorable kidney and vasopressor outcomes. This evidence supports the clinical trend toward balanced physiologic crystalloids.[1.1083-1084]

Chloride-containing crystalloids, including normal saline, were losing favor because of hyperchloremia and metabolic acidosis.[2.951]

Albumin

Some albumin may be considered in septic patients with significant hypoalbuminemia and risk of multiple-organ failure.[1.1236]

No general mortality advantage for albumin over crystalloid is demonstrated in most ICU resuscitation. Selected settings where albumin plus crystalloid may have benefit include cirrhosis with spontaneous bacterial peritonitis; possible benefit in severe burns is also noted, while crystalloids remain first-line for most septic shock.[1.1081-1083]

Hydroxyethyl starch

Strong evidence of harm from HES in critical illness is presented. In sepsis, HES was associated with higher 90-day mortality and greater renal-replacement-therapy use than Ringer’s acetate in the cited trial. Across larger analyses, HES was associated with increased AKI and mortality. HES should be avoided because of higher complication rates, particularly AKI.[1.1082-1086]

Packed red blood cells and hemoglobin targets

A randomized septic-shock comparison of hemoglobin thresholds of 7 versus 9 g/dL found that the higher-threshold group received more units but did not have a mortality advantage at 90 days, and there was no significant difference in ischemic events, severe reactions, or life-support use. Transfusing above 7 g/dL does not appear to add significant benefit to crystalloids alone for most sepsis-related hypoperfusion once the initial resuscitation context is considered.[1.1084]

The older EGDT algorithm is different: when ScvO2 remains below 70%, it instructs transfusion when hemoglobin is below 8 g/dL, together with consideration of dobutamine when cardiac index is below 3.5 L/min/m2.[3.27]

Fluid overload and de-resuscitation

Excessive positive fluid balance after initial resuscitation is associated with worse outcomes. In severe sepsis/septic shock treated with early goal-directed therapy, volume overload was associated with more fluid-related interventions and hospital death. Other sepsis cohorts associated positive fluid balance and high CVP with mortality, while a more conservative post-resuscitation balance was associated with better outcomes in selected analyses.[1.1084-1085]

At the same time, 1 warns that simply restricting fluid aggressively can also cause harm. There is no universal formula; the clinician must balance adequate resuscitation against edema, AKI, lung injury, and other consequences of overload.[1.1085]

After initial resuscitation and stabilization, a negative fluid balance may be beneficial.[3.28]

P - Pump: vasopressors and inotropes

Norepinephrine

Norepinephrine is the preferred first-line vasopressor when fluid administration alone does not restore adequate perfusion pressure. Dopamine has more adverse effects, particularly arrhythmias, and a cited meta-analysis associated dopamine with higher mortality than norepinephrine in septic shock.[1.1236]

Dopamine

Dopamine stimulates adrenergic and dopaminergic receptors. the clinical relevance of its renal and splanchnic vasodilator effects is doubtful. Because of arrhythmias and worse outcome signals, it is not the preferred vasopressor in the septic-shock chapter.[1.1236]

Epinephrine

Epinephrine should not be used as the first-line vasopressor in septic shock. It may adversely affect splanchnic circulation and increase cellular metabolism.[1.1236]

Dobutamine

Dobutamine is used for its positive inotropic effect when an increase in cardiac output is needed. It is described as an add-on to vasopressor therapy, especially when myocardial dysfunction or inadequate output persists.[1.1236]

Vasopressin

Patients with septic shock may have relative vasopressin deficiency. Excessive vasoconstriction may reduce splanchnic and coronary blood flow, and vasopressin should not be given when cardiac output is low. The dose in this material should not exceed 0.03 U/min, and cardiac output should be monitored.[1.1236]

Vasopressin 0.04 U/min is listed in the septic-shock medication table. This numerical difference is deliberately preserved rather than harmonized.[3.26]

Angiotensin II

Angiotensin II has strong vasopressor activity. Renewed interest in the agent is described, but its benefits need better definition before generalized use can be recommended.[1.1236]

Oxygen delivery, ScvO2, and transfusion logic

Maintaining oxygen delivery and cardiac output is fundamental. Because septic shock is often hyperdynamic, mixed venous oxygen saturation may be normal or high.[1.1236]

1 says that attempting to maintain ScvO2 at or above 70% may be useful in selected patients but should not be routine. In a patient who is not improving after initial resuscitation, a low ScvO2 should trigger reassessment; additional fluid and possibly dobutamine may be considered, and transfusion may be useful in selected circumstances.[1.1236]

Early goal-directed therapy: evolution inside the source set

The original Rivers trial is described, in which an intensive 6-hour goal-directed strategy that used fluids, transfusion, vasopressors, inotropes, and tissue-oxygenation targets was associated with a 16% absolute reduction in 28-day mortality.[3.26]

The later ProMISe, ARISE, and ProCESS trials did not show improved outcomes from a fixed protocolized EGDT strategy compared with contemporary standard care. The discussion notes that the later control groups were less severely ill than the original Rivers cohort and that standard care had already incorporated many early-resuscitation elements.[3.28]

The same evolution is described in the dedicated fluid material: the Rivers-era protocol used 500-mL crystalloid boluses every 30 minutes with a CVP target of 8-12 mmHg and other oxygen-delivery goals, but subsequent ARISE, ProCESS, and ProMISe trials did not reproduce a survival benefit from the fixed EGDT protocol.[1.1080-1081]

The integrated source message is therefore not that the original fixed EGDT targets remain universally required. The common element preserved across editions is early recognition and timely delivery of the therapies actually needed: appropriate antimicrobials, source control, individualized fluid resuscitation, vasoactive support, and organ support.[3.26-28; 1.1080-1081]

Corticosteroids and adrenal dysfunction

This is one of the areas with the clearest edition-to-edition differences, so the sources must remain separate.

the older position

Corticosteroids are not effective as routine sepsis therapy but that steroid replacement is required when acute adrenal insufficiency is present.[4.201]

the earlier septic-shock chapter

Very high corticosteroid doses had not shown benefit. It discusses moderate-dose hydrocortisone, approximately 200 mg over 24 hours, for septic shock as a proposed therapy but describes the issue as debated in that edition.[2.1054]

the manual septic-shock table

Hydrocortisone 50 mg every 6 hours, with or without fludrocortisone 50 mcg daily, is listed; benefit is described as limited to patients with vasopressor-dependent septic shock despite adequate fluid resuscitation.[3.26-27]

Adrenal-insufficiency material: earlier manual

The Annane and CORTICUS experience and the diagnostic uncertainty of critical-illness-related corticosteroid insufficiency are reviewed. Septic-shock patients who remain hypotensive despite IV fluids and vasopressors can be started on hydrocortisone 200-300 mg/day for 5-7 days while the cause of adrenal dysfunction is evaluated.[3.190]

Adrenal insufficiency is considered likely with very low baseline cortisol values (approximately below 10-15 mcg/dL in the discussion), and some clinicians use a random cortisol threshold of 25 mcg/dL to regard adrenal insufficiency as unlikely. A delta cortisol of 9 mcg/dL or less after 250 mcg cosyntropin is also discussed as a possible marker, while acknowledging limitations of cortisol testing in critical illness.[3.189-191]

the later septic-shock chapter

High-dose corticosteroids are not beneficial, but moderate-dose corticosteroids may help in septic shock. Hydrocortisone 200 mg in 24 hours is given as the moderate-dose approach discussed.[1.1236]

Adrenal-insufficiency material: later textbook

A more detailed regimen for catecholamine-resistant septic shock is: 100 mg initial hydrocortisone bolus followed by 200-300 mg/day, with tapering after stabilization.[1.1498-1499]

Hydrocortisone has intrinsic mineralocorticoid activity. Conflicting evidence is discussed regarding adding fludrocortisone: one trial showed improved survival with combined glucocorticoid/mineralocorticoid therapy in vasopressor-resistant shock, while another cited trial did not demonstrate additional benefit from oral fludrocortisone.[1.1498-1499]

Low-dose hydrocortisone can raise serum sodium, and hypernatremia above 155 mmol/L has been reported during prolonged treatment. Therapy should be limited to the period of volume- and vasopressor-resistant hypotension.[1.1498]

A possible weight-adjusted continuous dose of 0.18 mg/kg/h is also discussed, but superiority over fixed dosing is not established and bolus versus continuous infusion has not been definitively compared. Gradual weaning over several days is advised because abrupt discontinuation can produce hemodynamic and immunologic rebound.[1.1499]

Cortisol testing is discussed, but a preliminary ACTH test is no longer recommended as a requirement before giving low-dose hydrocortisone to the appropriate refractory-shock patient. Basal cortisol below 15 mcg/dL is listed as a level at which replacement is recommended, above 34 mcg/dL as sufficient, and a less than 9 mcg/dL incremental rise between those ranges as supportive of relative adrenal insufficiency; alternative cutoff proposals are also acknowledged.[1.1499]

Most importantly, the studies supporting steroid use were performed in catecholamine-resistant septic shock, and there are no data in this material to justify low-dose steroids for sepsis without shock.[1.1499]

Fever management

Treating fever in septic shock is controversial. Higher temperature increases oxygen demand, but fever may also participate in host defense. In the cited multicenter ICU study, acetaminophen was tolerated but did not reduce mortality.[1.1236]

Nutrition and glucose

Malnutrition can prolong sepsis and increase complications. Nutritional advice includes:

  • no urgent nutrition unless the patient is already malnourished,
  • preference for the enteral route over parenteral nutrition,
  • avoidance of enteral feeding during the unstable initial resuscitation phase,
  • initiation of enteral nutrition once hemodynamic stability is achieved, generally by 24-48 hours,
  • no need to start parenteral nutrition immediately; waiting several days is acceptable.[1.1236]

For glucose, 1 suggests keeping glucose below 180 mg/dL, and preferably below 150 mg/dL, while also avoiding large variability.[1.1236]

A narrower suggested target was 110-150 mg/dL and warned that overly strict control can cause hypoglycemia. This difference is preserved as an edition change rather than merged into one number.[2.1054]

Organ dysfunction and organ support

Respiratory failure and ARDS

Respiratory failure is common and is usually hypoxemic. ARDS is identified when PaO2/FiO2 is below 300 with bilateral infiltrates and no evidence that left-heart failure explains the picture. No specific ARDS therapy is given within this section; management depends on respiratory support and treatment of the underlying cause, such as sepsis.[1.1236-1237]

When mechanical ventilation is started, worsening arterial hypotension can indicate residual hypovolemia because positive intrathoracic pressure reduces venous return. Large tidal volumes and high inflation pressures should be avoided to reduce hemodynamic stress and ventilator-induced lung injury.[1.1236-1237]

Minimizing sedation when possible and using effective analgesia to reduce sedative requirements are also advised. This is summarized as the eCASH concept: early Comfort using Analgesia, minimal Sedatives, and maximal Humane care.[1.1237]

Acute kidney injury

Sepsis is described as a leading cause of acute renal failure/AKI in the ICU. Renal dysfunction results from both circulatory changes and inflammation, and can be worsened by nephrotoxins such as aminoglycosides or radiographic contrast.[1.1237]

Prevention centers on maintaining adequate renal perfusion and appropriate volume state. Dopaminergic “renal-dose” therapy does not prevent renal failure, and indiscriminate diuretic use may be harmful.[1.1237]

Renal-replacement therapy is frequently needed. In unstable septic shock, continuous venovenous hemofiltration, with or without dialysis, is described as generally preferable to intermittent techniques for easier control of fluid balance.[1.1237]

Sepsis-AKI prevention also includes close monitoring of serum creatinine and urine output, avoidance or dose adjustment of nephrotoxic agents, avoidance of HES, early control of infection, and adequate resuscitation without excessive fluid administration.[1.1092]

Coagulation abnormalities and SIC/DIC

Coagulopathy is common. Thrombocytopenia may be accompanied by prolonged PT and aPTT. Treatment focuses mainly on correcting the cause. Heparin is not indicated simply to treat the septic coagulopathy, apart from an appropriate indication such as thromboembolism prophylaxis. In severe bleeding, fresh frozen plasma or platelet transfusion may be needed.[1.1237]

Randomized trials of heparin, antithrombin, protein C, thrombomodulin, or tissue-factor-pathway inhibitor for presumed sepsis-induced coagulopathy/DIC have not demonstrated a mortality benefit. The primary management is treatment of the underlying sepsis.[1.178]

Hepatic dysfunction

Shock can raise liver-associated enzymes. Bilirubin may increase after several days even when there is no hemolysis, large hematoma, or primary biliary disease. Ultrasound or other testing may be needed to exclude separate biliary pathology.[1.1237]

Septic encephalopathy

Cerebral dysfunction often begins as confusion rather than coma. Prolonged cognitive change may be called septic encephalopathy. The exact mechanism is not defined. Benzodiazepines and other sedatives can contribute to delirium. EEG typically shows diffuse slowing, whereas CT and CSF examination may remain normal. The neurologic changes are usually reversible when shock resolves.[1.1237]

Immunomodulation and extracorporeal blood purification

Conventional immunomodulatory drugs

Therapies aimed at individual inflammatory pathways, including anti-TNF strategies and IL-1 receptor antagonism, have not produced convincing outcome benefits. Activated protein C (drotrecogin alfa) appeared beneficial in early studies but was withdrawn after a later negative placebo-controlled study. Thrombomodulin showed promising findings in sepsis-associated coagulopathy but required further confirmation in the 2024 text.[1.1236]

Intravenous therapies targeting pathways such as IFN-gamma, recombinant IL-7, and TNF-alpha failed to show morbidity or mortality benefit. One proposed explanation is the biological heterogeneity of sepsis and the limitation of targeting one immune pathway.[1.1071]

Principle of extracorporeal blood purification

Blood purification is described as nonspecific removal of circulating targets such as endotoxin, cytokines, inflammatory cells, or pathogens. Most techniques use adsorption, diffusion, convection, or a combination.[1.1071-1073]

Polymyxin-B hemoperfusion (Toraymyxin)

Polymyxin-B binds endotoxin but cannot be given systemically for this purpose because of renal and neurologic toxicity. In the Toraymyxin cartridge it is covalently bound to fibers. A typical treatment is described as two 2-hour hemoperfusion sessions 24 hours apart, and this has been used as standard care for abdominal septic shock in Japan.[1.1072-1073]

Early analyses reported improvements in MAP, oxygenation, vasopressor use, and mortality. EUPHAS reported a significant 28-day mortality signal and was stopped early. ABDOMIX did not reproduce the benefit. The larger EUPHRATES trial in patients with high endotoxin activity did not reduce 28-day mortality overall; a post-hoc subgroup with endotoxin activity between 0.6 and 0.9 had lower mortality. patient selection and timing remain unresolved.[1.1073]

Alteco LPS adsorber

The Alteco device uses a synthetic peptide with affinity for the lipid A portion of endotoxin. In the small nonrandomized study cited by 1, endotoxin activity and some clinical measures improved but ICU mortality did not differ. A feasibility trial was stopped because of recruitment problems.[1.1073]

High-volume hemofiltration and high-cutoff membranes

High-volume hemofiltration is defined as CRRT with an ultrafiltration rate above 50 mL/kg/h, intended to increase removal of middle-molecular-weight mediators such as cytokines. The IVOIRE trial and later meta-analyses did not demonstrate a significant mortality reduction.[1.1073-1074]

High-cutoff membranes can remove larger inflammatory mediators such as IL-6 and IL-1 but can cause albumin loss. Small observational data were encouraging, but randomized trials are still needed for septic AKI.[1.1073]

PMMA membrane

Polymethylmethacrylate membranes have cytokine and immunoglobulin-light-chain adsorption properties. Studies reported improvements in intermediate outcomes such as hemodynamics, lactate, and IL-6, but clotting and clogging were major problems. A modified PMMA membrane was designed to reduce thrombogenicity; the modified version had not yet been adequately studied in septic AKI.[1.1073-1074]

Coupled plasma filtration and adsorption (CPFA)

CPFA separates plasma, passes it through a nonspecific charcoal adsorber to remove cytokines, and then returns it to a circuit that also provides hemofiltration. The COMPACT2 trial was stopped early because of suspected increased mortality in the treatment group, and CPFA is no longer proposed for septic-shock management.[1.1074]

CytoSorb

CytoSorb uses polymer beads that adsorb small- to middle-molecular-weight hydrophobic molecules, including pro- and anti-inflammatory mediators, PAMPs, DAMPs, myoglobin, and bilirubin. It can be used alone or with RRT. Twenty-four-hour cartridges are described as being given daily for 2-7 days and notes observational suggestions that initiation within 24 hours of uncontrolled septic shock may be associated with better outcomes.[1.1074]

Randomized trials did not show a mortality reduction, although lactate and hemodynamic parameters improved. IL-6 clearance was demonstrated in one trial without a corresponding fall in circulating IL-6 concentration.[1.1074]

oXiris

oXiris is an RRT membrane designed to remove both cytokines and endotoxin and is heparin-grafted. Standard treatment sessions of about 24 hours are described, with possible filter life up to 72 hours. Small and mostly observational studies reported improved cytokine/endotoxin clearance, lactate, vasopressor need, hemodynamics, urine output, and PaO2/FiO2, but good-quality outcome trials are still needed.[1.1074]

Pathogen-removal and cell-removal devices

Seraph 100 is described as a heparin-grafted bead hemoperfusion device that can bind a range of bacteria, parasites, and viruses. A safety study in septic-shock patients requiring RRT found no adverse events, but clinical outcome benefit had not been established.[1.1074-1075]

Experimental Opsonix/FcMBL technology is also discussed as a method intended to bind whole pathogens or pathogen debris, together with the Hemopurifier, which uses plasmapheresis plus lectin adsorption to remove circulating viruses. These were described as investigational or research-stage technologies.[1.1074-1075]

Activated-leukocyte cytapheresis has also been studied. A preliminary safety study suggested improved survival compared with matched controls, but the later randomized trial did not confirm the finding.[1.1074]

Overall evidence for blood purification

Overall, blood-purification techniques can improve secondary physiologic outcomes such as hemodynamics or oxygenation, but none has conclusive randomized-trial evidence of survival benefit. Patient selection, timing, and matching the device to the biological target remain unresolved. Techniques that also provide RRT are most logical when the patient already requires RRT; standalone therapies may be more appropriate when AKI is absent.[1.1076]

Prognosis and multiple-organ dysfunction

Mortality is related to the severity of physiologic disturbance, duration of illness, and number of failing organ systems. Lung, kidney, and liver dysfunction are specifically mentioned, and multiple-organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS) is identified as a major mechanism of death when inflammation and infection remain uncontrolled.[4.201]

Persistently elevated lactate is associated with poor prognosis, and microcirculatory abnormalities are also associated with outcome.[1.1234-1237]

Renal function, CRRT, and context-specific dosing warning

Detailed antimicrobial dosing tables supply renal and CRRT adjustments for many of the gram-negative agents above, including piperacillin/tazobactam, ceftazidime, cefepime, ceftazidime/avibactam, ceftolozane/tazobactam, imipenem, meropenem, ertapenem, doripenem, aminoglycosides, ciprofloxacin, and levofloxacin. The same material stresses that augmented renal clearance, large-volume fluid resuscitation, hypoalbuminemia, vasopressors, effusions/ascites, and RRT can all change antimicrobial exposure. It defines augmented renal clearance in this discussion as an 8-hour creatinine clearance of at least 130 mL/min/1.73 m2.[1.1169-1171; 1.1133-1134]

Because the four books use site-specific regimens for pneumonia, abdominal sepsis, urinary infection, meningitis, catheter infection, and other syndromes, those site-specific doses must not be silently substituted for one another. For example, the abdominal-sepsis table gives its own regimens and doses, while pneumonia tables use different combinations, intervals, and infusion times. This chapter therefore preserves the general sepsis algorithm and provides a dose cross-reference, but it does not relabel a pneumonia or intraabdominal regimen as a universal sepsis regimen.[1.1005; 1.716; 1.736]
